Large language models can reproduce federal statistical data with high confidence from training data, web grounding, or API retrieval. This capability reflects significant agency investment in machine-readable formats, standardized metadata, and public APIs. Accurate data is not competent consultation. LLMs lack the expert judgment to assess whether data is fit for a specific use: evaluating margins of error, interpreting period estimates, or recognizing when an estimate is too unreliable to report. Standard Retrieval-Augmented Generation fails to bridge this gap. Domain-homogeneous methodology documentation clusters too tightly in embedding spaces, a phenomenon we term "semantic smearing," impeding precise retrieval of fitness-for-use guidance.

This paper introduces *pragmatics*: structured expert judgment delivered computationally at the point of statistical reasoning. In a knowledge representation study, an LLM was tested across 39 Census data queries under three conditions: no methodology support, standard RAG using 311 document chunks, and pragmatics using 36 curated items. Both treatments drew from the same 354 pages of Census Bureau source documentation. Only the representation method differed.

Pragmatics produced very large improvements in consultation quality over control, Cohen's $d=1.440$, rank-biserial $r=0.938$, and over RAG, $d=0.922$, $r=0.754$. The strongest effects were on uncertainty communication, $d=1.353$. Pipeline fidelity reached 91.2% versus 74.6% for RAG, with 100% deterministic context retrieval. Cost-effectiveness favored pragmatics at 2.2 times RAG per marginal dollar, at nine cents per query over baseline. Selective curation of expert judgment outperforms volume-based retrieval for grounding AI systems in statistical reasoning.
